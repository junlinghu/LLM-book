# 8.4 Instruction Data

The SFT loss copies whatever the demonstrations contain. A model fine-tuned on careful, correct, well-formatted responses learns to produce such responses; a model fine-tuned on sloppy ones learns to be sloppy. Data is therefore the main lever in supervised fine-tuning, and most of the effort in a real SFT project goes into it. This section surveys where instruction data comes from (people, existing datasets, and other models), what the evidence says about quality versus quantity, how reasoning traces fit in, and the cleaning steps that every dataset needs: deduplication, filtering, and decontamination.

## Human-written demonstrations

The most direct source is people writing the responses. InstructGPT's SFT data was written by a team of about 40 contractors, who wrote responses to prompts submitted by users of the OpenAI API and to prompts they wrote themselves; the SFT training set had about 13,000 prompts (Ouyang et al. 2022). The labelers followed detailed guidelines about what a good response looks like: helpful, truthful, and harmless, with particular attention to what the user most plausibly wanted.

Human demonstrations have clear strengths. They can cover exactly the requests the product will face, they encode the style and values the developers want, and a skilled writer can produce responses better than any existing model. Their weaknesses are cost and consistency. Writing a good response to a hard request, such as a careful explanation of a legal question or a correct proof, takes an expert a long time, and different writers make different choices about length, tone, and format. The model will learn the average of those choices unless the guidelines and review process make them consistent.

The Llama 2 authors reported a useful data point about the trade-off between quantity and quality (Touvron et al. 2023). They started with publicly available instruction data, found that much of it lacked diversity and quality, and instead collected their own high-quality annotations. They found that a limited set of clean examples worked better than millions of third-party examples, and stopped after collecting 27,540 annotations for SFT.

## Templated NLP datasets

A second source is the large collection of labeled datasets built for NLP research: sentiment classification, question answering, summarization, translation, natural language inference, and many more. Each dataset can be converted into instruction-response pairs by writing templates that phrase its task as an instruction. This is how FLAN (Wei et al. 2022a), T0 (Sanh et al. 2022), and Super-NaturalInstructions (Wang et al. 2022) were built, and the Flan collection scaled the approach to 1,836 tasks (Chung et al. 2024).

A small example shows the mechanics (Appendix A.1). Four labeled reviews, such as "The battery died after two days." labeled negative, combined with three templates give twelve instruction examples:

| Template | Example prompt | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Question first | "Is the sentiment of this review positive or negative?" ⏎⏎ "Best concert I have been to in years!" | positive |
| Question last, different label words | "The food was wonderful and the staff were friendly." ⏎⏎ "Did the writer like the experience? Answer yes or no." | yes |
| Structured | "Classify the review as positive or negative." ⏎ "Review: The battery died after two days." ⏎ "Sentiment:" | negative |

Using several templates per dataset matters. A model trained on a single phrasing may learn to recognize the phrasing rather than the task; varied phrasings, including ones where the label words change ("yes" and "no" instead of "positive" and "negative"), push it to understand the instruction. The Flan collection study found that mixing prompts in several formats, including zero-shot prompts, prompts with a few examples, and prompts that ask for chain-of-thought reasoning, improved results in all of these settings (Longpre et al. 2023).

Templated datasets are cheap, large, and come with correct answers. Their weakness is that their tasks and responses look like benchmarks: short labels, extractive answers, formal inputs. A model trained only on them follows instructions well in a narrow sense, but writes terse, unhelpful answers to open-ended requests. They are best used as one part of a mixture.

## Data generated by models

The third source, and by volume the largest today, is data written by language models. The idea was demonstrated by **Self-Instruct** (Wang et al. 2023). Starting from 175 human-written seed tasks, the method repeatedly prompts a pretrained model with a few examples from the task pool and asks it to write new instructions, then to write inputs and outputs for them. Each new instruction is added to the pool only if it is sufficiently different from every instruction already there, measured by ROUGE-L, a similarity score based on the longest common subsequence of words; instructions with a similarity of 0.7 or more to any existing one are discarded. The procedure produced about 52,000 instructions from the original 175.

The similarity filter is easy to implement, and trying it shows both why it is useful and what it misses (Appendix A.3). With a pool of three tasks ("Write a short poem about the ocean.", "Explain how a rainbow forms.", "List three uses of baking soda."), four generated candidates score as follows:

| Candidate | Highest ROUGE-L against the pool | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Write a short poem about the moon. | 0.86 | drop |
| Explain how a volcano forms. | 0.80 | drop |
| Describe the rules of chess in simple terms. | 0.14 | keep |
| List three uses of vinegar. | 0.73 | drop |

The filter removes near-copies of the same template, which a generating model produces constantly. It also drops "Explain how a volcano forms.", a genuinely different task that happens to share most of its words with an existing one, while it would keep a paraphrase that uses different words for the same request. Word-overlap filters measure surface form, not meaning. Production pipelines often add filters based on embeddings (Chapter 4), which compare meaning more directly, and filters that use a strong model to judge the quality of each example.

A common variant uses a stronger model, the **teacher**, to write the responses for a smaller model, the **student**. This is a form of **sequence-level knowledge distillation** (Kim and Rush 2016): instead of matching the teacher's probability distribution token by token, the student is trained on the teacher's outputs as if they were human demonstrations. It is cheap and effective, and a large share of open instruction data is produced this way.

## The limits of imitation

Distillation has a trap. A study of small open models fine-tuned on outputs of a much stronger proprietary model found that human raters initially rated the imitation models' answers highly, because they sounded like the teacher's (Gudibande et al. 2023). Targeted evaluations told a different story: on tasks that were not heavily represented in the imitation data, the imitation models closed little of the gap to the teacher in factual accuracy and capability. They had learned the teacher's style (confident, well structured, fluent) much more than its substance.

This is the superficial alignment hypothesis of Section 1 seen from the other side. If SFT mostly teaches format and style, then imitating a stronger model's outputs will transfer its format and style easily, and its knowledge and reasoning only to the extent that the student's pretraining already supports them. The authors concluded that improving the base model is a stronger lever than imitation data. For practitioners, the lesson is to evaluate distilled models on held-out tasks with checkable answers, not only on how their answers read.

## Reasoning traces

Some responses show their work. A **chain of thought**, a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps before the final answer, was first used as a prompting technique: including worked examples with reasoning in the prompt made large models much better at arithmetic and logic problems (Wei et al. 2022b). The same traces can be used as SFT data. A model fine-tuned on responses that reason step by step learns to reason step by step before answering, and this does teach something beyond style, because the intermediate steps make hard problems easier to solve one token at a time.

The strongest demonstration of this so far is distillation from reasoning models, which are trained to produce long chains of thought. The DeepSeek-R1 authors fine-tuned several smaller open models, from the Qwen2.5 and Llama families, on about 800,000 samples curated with DeepSeek-R1, using SFT alone, and the resulting models were far better at math and coding benchmarks than their starting points (DeepSeek-AI 2025). Reasoning traces are only useful if they are correct, so such data is usually filtered by checking the final answer: math problems against their known solutions, and code against unit tests.

## Quality, diversity, and the mixture

Several findings point in the same direction. LIMA reached strong results with 1,000 carefully chosen examples (Zhou et al. 2023a). Llama 2's SFT stage preferred tens of thousands of clean annotations to millions of noisy ones (Touvron et al. 2023). Imitation data transfers style more easily than capability (Gudibande et al. 2023). The common thread is that, beyond a modest size, the quality and diversity of SFT data matter more than its quantity.

**Quality** means that responses are correct, complete, well formatted, and consistent in style. A single wrong answer in a small dataset is learned just as thoroughly as a right one.

**Diversity** means covering the range of requests the model should handle: different tasks, topics, lengths, formats, languages, levels of difficulty, and conversation lengths. Diversity is also what makes a model robust to phrasings it has never seen.

**The mixture** is the proportion of each kind of data. Open post-training recipes such as Tulu 3 describe their SFT data as a curated mix of sources targeting specific skills (general chat, knowledge recall, math, coding, instruction following, safety, and multilingual ability), and they tune the proportions by measuring how each change affects evaluations of every skill (Lambert et al. 2024). Adding more math data, for instance, can improve math while degrading something else, and only an evaluation across all target skills reveals the trade-off.

## Deduplication

Duplicates are common in instruction data, especially in data generated by models and data collected from many public sources. They waste compute, and repeated examples are learned more strongly than the rest, which skews the model toward whatever happened to be duplicated. Section 7.3 discussed deduplication of pretraining data (Lee et al. 2022); the same tools apply here, at a much smaller scale.

**Exact duplicates** are found by hashing each example after normalizing it, for example by lowercasing, collapsing whitespace, and removing punctuation. In Appendix A.2, "Write a short poem about the ocean!" and "write a short  poem about the ocean." hash to the same value as "Write a short poem about the ocean." after normalization.

**Near duplicates** are found by comparing sets of word n-grams. The Jaccard similarity of two sets is the size of their intersection divided by the size of their union. Among the six distinct prompts that remain in the example, only one pair shares any word 3-gram: "Write a short poem about the ocean." and "Write a short poem about the sea at night." have a Jaccard similarity of 0.50. The paraphrase pairs "Explain how a rainbow forms." and "Explain how rainbows form in the sky.", and "List three uses of baking soda." and "Give me three ways to use baking soda around the house.", share no 3-gram at all and score 0. As with the ROUGE-L filter, n-gram methods catch copies and template reuse, not paraphrases. At scale, MinHash approximates Jaccard similarity efficiently for millions of examples, and embedding similarity catches paraphrases at a higher cost.

## Decontamination

Instruction data must not contain the test sets that will be used to evaluate the model. Contamination happens easily: benchmark questions are published online and end up in scraped data, and a teacher model asked to generate "math word problems" may reproduce problems it memorized from a benchmark. A contaminated model scores well on the benchmark without having the ability the benchmark is supposed to measure.

A standard check looks for long word sequences shared between training examples and test items. The GPT-3 authors, for example, flagged overlaps of 13-word sequences between their training data and benchmarks (Brown et al. 2020). In Appendix A.4, a training example that embeds a benchmark's train-and-station word problem, with a short instruction before it and "Show your work." after it, shares 14 13-grams with the benchmark item and is flagged. A structurally similar problem about a bus, with different numbers and wording, shares none. The check is cheap and catches copied questions reliably, but it cannot catch a benchmark problem that has been paraphrased or translated, and it says nothing about whether training on many similar problems inflates a score. Careful evaluation reports both the decontamination method and results on held-out data created after the training data was collected.

## Licenses and terms

Instruction data comes with legal and ethical conditions. Human-written data may contain personal information that must be removed. Public datasets come with licenses that may restrict commercial use. Data generated by a proprietary model may be subject to that model's terms of service, some of which restrict using outputs to develop competing models. These questions do not affect how SFT works, but they affect which data a project can use, and they are much easier to handle when data sources are recorded from the start.

## Key takeaways

- Instruction data comes from human demonstrations (high quality, costly, tailored), templated NLP datasets (cheap, correct, but benchmark-like), and model-generated data (cheap and flexible, now the largest source).
- Templated data should use several phrasings and formats per task, so the model learns the task rather than the template.
- Self-Instruct bootstraps tens of thousands of instructions from a small seed set with a similarity filter; word-overlap filters remove copies but also drop some genuinely new tasks and miss paraphrases.
- Distilling a stronger model's outputs transfers its style more easily than its knowledge and capability, so distilled models must be evaluated on held-out tasks with checkable answers.
- Reasoning traces teach step-by-step reasoning, and filtering them by the correctness of the final answer is essential.
- Beyond a modest size, the quality and diversity of SFT data matter more than its quantity, and the mixture of skills must be tuned against evaluations of every skill.
- Every dataset needs deduplication (exact hashing and n-gram similarity) and decontamination against evaluation sets (for example, 13-gram overlap), with an understanding of what these checks miss.

### A.1 Instruction templates for a labeled dataset

Turn four labeled sentiment examples into instruction-response pairs with three templates, in the style of FLAN.

```python
import random, re, hashlib
from collections import Counter

labeled = [("The food was wonderful and the staff were friendly.", "positive"),
           ("I waited an hour and my order was wrong.", "negative"),
           ("Best concert I have been to in years!", "positive"),
           ("The battery died after two days.", "negative")]
templates = [
    ("Is the sentiment of this review positive or negative?\n\n{text}", "{label}"),
    ("{text}\n\nDid the writer like the experience? Answer yes or no.", "{yesno}"),
    ("Classify the review as positive or negative.\nReview: {text}\nSentiment:", "{label}"),
]
random.seed(0)
examples = []
for text, label in labeled:
    for prompt_t, answer_t in templates:
        fields = {"text": text, "label": label, "yesno": "yes" if label == "positive" else "no"}
        examples.append({"prompt": prompt_t.format(**fields), "response": answer_t.format(**fields)})
print(len(examples), "instruction examples from", len(labeled), "labeled examples")
for ex in random.sample(examples, 2):
    print("---\nPROMPT:", ex["prompt"], "\nRESPONSE:", ex["response"])
```

Output:

```
12 instruction examples from 4 labeled examples
---
PROMPT: Is the sentiment of this review positive or negative?

Best concert I have been to in years! 
RESPONSE: positive
---
PROMPT: Classify the review as positive or negative.
Review: The battery died after two days.
Sentiment: 
RESPONSE: negative
```

### A.2 Exact and near-duplicate detection

Find exact duplicates after normalization, and near duplicates by the Jaccard similarity of word 3-grams.

```python
pool = [
    "Write a short poem about the ocean.",
    "Write a short poem about the ocean!",
    "write a short  poem about the ocean.",
    "Write a short poem about the sea at night.",
    "Explain how a rainbow forms.",
    "Explain how rainbows form in the sky.",
    "List three uses of baking soda.",
    "Give me three ways to use baking soda around the house.",
]

def normalize(s):
    return re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9 ]", "", re.sub(r"\s+", " ", s.lower())).strip()

seen, unique = {}, []
for s in pool:
    h = hashlib.sha1(normalize(s).encode()).hexdigest()
    if h in seen:
        print(f"exact duplicate: {s!r} of {seen[h]!r}")
    else:
        seen[h] = s
        unique.append(s)

def shingles(s, n=3):
    w = normalize(s).split()
    return {tuple(w[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(w) - n + 1)}

def jaccard(a, b):
    A, B = shingles(a), shingles(b)
    return len(A & B) / len(A | B) if A | B else 0.0

pairs = [(jaccard(a, b), a, b) for i, a in enumerate(unique) for b in unique[i + 1:]]
for score, a, b in sorted(p for p in pairs if p[0] > 0):
    print(f"{score:.2f}  {a!r} vs {b!r}")
print(len(pairs), "pairs checked;", sum(s > 0 for s, _, _ in pairs), "share any word 3-gram")
```

Output:

```
exact duplicate: 'Write a short poem about the ocean!' of 'Write a short poem about the ocean.'
exact duplicate: 'write a short  poem about the ocean.' of 'Write a short poem about the ocean.'
0.50  'Write a short poem about the ocean.' vs 'Write a short poem about the sea at night.'
15 pairs checked; 1 share any word 3-gram
```

### A.3 The Self-Instruct similarity filter

Keep a generated instruction only if its ROUGE-L similarity to every instruction already in the pool is below 0.7.

```python
def lcs(a, b):
    dp = [[0] * (len(b) + 1) for _ in range(len(a) + 1)]
    for i, x in enumerate(a):
        for j, y in enumerate(b):
            dp[i + 1][j + 1] = dp[i][j] + 1 if x == y else max(dp[i][j + 1], dp[i + 1][j])
    return dp[-1][-1]

def rouge_l(a, b):
    a, b = normalize(a).split(), normalize(b).split()
    l = lcs(a, b)
    if l == 0:
        return 0.0
    p, r = l / len(b), l / len(a)
    return 2 * p * r / (p + r)

task_pool = ["Write a short poem about the ocean.", "Explain how a rainbow forms.",
             "List three uses of baking soda."]
candidates = ["Write a short poem about the moon.",
              "Explain how a volcano forms.",
              "Describe the rules of chess in simple terms.",
              "List three uses of vinegar."]
for c in candidates:
    score = max(rouge_l(c, t) for t in task_pool)
    keep = score < 0.7
    print(f"{score:.2f} {'keep' if keep else 'drop'}  {c!r}")
    if keep:
        task_pool.append(c)
```

Output:

```
0.86 drop  'Write a short poem about the moon.'
0.80 drop  'Explain how a volcano forms.'
0.14 keep  'Describe the rules of chess in simple terms.'
0.73 drop  'List three uses of vinegar.'
```

### A.4 Decontamination by 13-gram overlap

Flag training examples that share any 13-word sequence with a benchmark question.

```python
def ngrams(s, n=13):
    w = normalize(s).split()
    return {tuple(w[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(w) - n + 1)}

benchmark = ["A train leaves the station at 3 pm traveling at 60 miles per hour toward a "
             "city 180 miles away. At what time does it arrive?"]
train = [
    "Solve this: a train leaves the station at 3 pm traveling at 60 miles per hour toward a "
    "city 180 miles away. At what time does it arrive? Show your work.",
    "A bus leaves at 9 am traveling at 40 miles per hour toward a town 120 miles away. "
    "When does it arrive?",
    "Explain why the sky is blue in two sentences.",
]
bench_grams = set().union(*(ngrams(b) for b in benchmark))
for t in train:
    hits = len(ngrams(t) & bench_grams)
    print(f"{hits:2d} shared 13-grams  {'CONTAMINATED' if hits else 'clean':12s} {t[:50]!r}...")
```
